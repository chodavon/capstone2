Would this email fool you?

Read the sample. Then tap each red flag to see what gives it away. No real bank or brand is used.

Inbox / 1 new message
From

MekongPay Support <support@mekongpay-secure.example>

To

grandtesting168@gmail.com

Subject

Urgent: unusual sign-in on your account

Dear customer,

We blocked a sign-in from a new device. To keep your account active, confirm your identity within 2 hours.

Verify your account

If you did not request this, ignore this email.

MekongPay Customer Care

Link preview

https://mekongpay-secure.example/verify

Spot the tell

0 of 5 found

  • The domain adds extra words before the real name. A real bank writes from its official domain, not a lookalike.

  • “Urgent” and “unusual sign-in” are designed to make you act before you check. Real security alerts do not need to frighten you.

  • “Dear customer” means the same message was sent to thousands of people. A real bank can use your name.

  • Two hours is meant to stop you from calling the bank or checking the app. Slow down. Verify through a channel you chose.

  • The link does not go to the bank’s app or official site. Do not tap it. Open the app yourself, or type the address you know.

This sample is fictional. “MekongPay” is not a real service.

Case 01 Account takeover 12 Nov 2026

The unlocked laptop that gave away a Telegram session

A five-minute walk from the desk was enough. The machine was unlocked, Telegram Desktop was signed in, and the session files on disk did the rest.

  • 4 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

An employee stepped outside “for a few minutes” and left the computer unlocked. Telegram Desktop was open and already signed in. An attacker nearby did not need a password: the session files stored on the machine were enough to reuse the account from another computer and appear as the same user.

From there, the attacker read private conversations and sent a friend a short message: “Can you send me some money? I have an urgent problem.” When the employee returned, the account showed a device they did not recognise.

The friend nearly paid. The employee spent the next hour explaining that the message was not theirs.

The turning point

“I’ll be right back.” The screen stayed unlocked, and the desktop session stayed active.

Red flags to recognise

  • An unlocked computer with an authenticated desktop app.
  • A new or unfamiliar device in the account’s active sessions.
  • Contacts receiving urgent money requests that do not sound like you.
  • A message that pushes for speed and secrecy.

What to do

  1. Lock the screen every time you leave: Windows, Win+L; macOS, Ctrl+Cmd+Q.
  2. Open Settings → Devices in Telegram and end every session you do not recognise.
  3. Turn on two-step verification so a copied session is not enough on its own.
  4. Tell IT and warn your contacts from a trusted device.
An open session is a spare key. Lock the screen, and check your devices.

Case 02 Phishing 9 Nov 2026

The reward message that ended at a fake login page

A congratulatory message, a lookalike login form, and one verification code. That was all it took to lose the account.

  • 3 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

A message arrived on Telegram: “Congratulations! You have been selected to receive a special reward. Click here to claim it.” The link opened a page that looked exactly like the messaging service’s login screen.

It asked for a username, a password, and a verification code. The victim entered all three. The page then failed and closed, but the credentials had already reached the attacker.

The attacker signed in, changed the password, and sent urgent money requests to the victim’s contacts. The victim found out when a friend called to ask whether the message was real.

The turning point

The page asked for a password and a one-time code in the same breath. A real service does not collect both from a link in a chat.

Red flags to recognise

  • A prize you never entered for.
  • A link that opens a login page.
  • A request for your password and verification code together.
  • A deadline designed to stop you checking.
  • A domain that is close to, but not exactly, the real one.

What to do

  1. Never sign in from a link in a message. Open the app or type the address yourself.
  2. Check the domain before you type anything. Look for extra words, hyphens, or unusual endings.
  3. Turn on two-step verification and keep the recovery details offline.
  4. If you entered details: change the password from a trusted device, end unknown sessions, warn contacts, and report it to IT.
A login page reached through a message is not a login page you can trust.

Case 03 Malware 6 Nov 2026

The CV that was really a program

A job application arrived as CV.pdf.exe. Windows showed only CV.pdf. HR opened it.

  • 4 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

An HR employee received a job application with an attachment named CV.pdf.exe. Windows had file extensions hidden, so the file appeared as CV.pdf, with the icon of a document.

The employee opened it. The file was a program, and it installed spyware. The computer slowed down, an unfamiliar application appeared, and the attacker could begin collecting documents and watching activity.

The connection was made only later: the “CV” was not a document at all.

The turning point

The file was an executable wearing a document’s name. The icon looked right; the extension told the truth.

Red flags to recognise

  • A document attachment that is really .exe, .scr, .js, .lnk, or another executable type.
  • File extensions hidden by Windows.
  • An unsolicited CV or invoice with pressure to open it quickly.
  • A sudden slowdown, unknown app, or unusual network activity.

What to do

  1. Show file extensions: in File Explorer, View → Show → File name extensions.
  2. Never run an attachment from an unknown sender. Ask the applicant to send a PDF through a known channel.
  3. Inspect the file in a sandbox or with IT. Treat a zero-detection scan as one data point, not proof of safety.
  4. If you opened it: disconnect from the network, stop using the machine, and report it to IT.
A document name is not proof of what a file does.

Case 04 Data exposure 3 Nov 2026

The office photo that mapped the office

A public post showed a nice office. The file underneath it also showed the latitude and longitude.

  • 3 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

An employee photographed the office and posted it publicly. A colleague suggested being careful. The attacker downloaded the image and read its EXIF metadata: GPS coordinates, the time it was taken, and the device model.

The coordinates led to the company building. The attacker used that detail to send an employee a convincing fake delivery notification. The message knew the office address, so it felt real.

The employee almost clicked. The detail that made the message convincing came from a photo, not from the company.

The turning point

The photo was not just a picture. It carried a location and a timestamp the poster never meant to share.

Red flags to recognise

  • Location tagging left on for the camera or social app.
  • Public photos showing badges, whiteboards, screens, keys, or visitor logs.
  • A delivery message you did not expect, especially one that knows your office address.

What to do

  1. Turn off location access for camera and social apps, or remove metadata before posting.
  2. Check the background before you post: screens, documents, badges, and people.
  3. Review who can see your posts, and remove old public photos that reveal the office.
  4. Verify unexpected delivery messages through the courier’s official app or a number you already know.
A photo can carry more than the picture. Check it before you post.

Case 05 Ransomware 30 Oct 2026

The invoice that encrypted the shared drive

A supplier invoice looked routine. It encrypted local files and the shared folder, then left a ransom note.

  • 4 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

An employee received an email that looked like a supplier invoice and opened the attachment. Documents stopped opening. A ransom note appeared, and the shared company folder became inaccessible.

Ransomware encrypts files quickly and spreads to connected drives. Because the shared folder was mapped to the computer, the damage was not limited to one desk.

The manager arrived asking why the team could not open its files. The answer was already on the screen.

The turning point

One attachment, one mapped drive. The invoice was not an invoice; it was the delivery method.

Red flags to recognise

  • An unexpected invoice, even from a supplier you know.
  • Pressure to open or pay quickly.
  • A macro-enabled document or an executable attachment.
  • Files renamed with unfamiliar extensions, or a note demanding payment.
  • Errors opening shared drives.

What to do

  1. Verify every invoice through a phone number you already have, not the reply-to address in the email.
  2. Do not open attachments you did not expect. Ask the supplier to confirm through a separate channel.
  3. Keep offline or immutable backups and test a restore before you need one.
  4. If it happens: disconnect the machine from the network, do not pay, report to IT, and follow the incident plan.
Backups decide how a ransomware story ends. Test them first.

Case 06 Social engineering 27 Oct 2026

The CV that waited in the downloads folder

The employee did the right thing, then forgot it. Days later, the file was still there.

  • 5 min read
  • Fictional scenario
  • Defensive briefing

A stranger on Telegram sent a CV and asked for it to be reviewed. The file was CV.pdf.exe, but Windows displayed CV.pdf. The employee did not open it and checked it with VirusTotal first.

The result was zero detections. A colleague warned that zero is not a guarantee, especially for new or custom malware. The employee left the file in Downloads and moved on.

Days later, while tidying the folder, they double-clicked “CV.pdf”. The machine began behaving strangely.

The turning point

The warning was followed, then forgotten. The dangerous file was never deleted.

Red flags to recognise

  • A double extension such as .pdf.exe, or file extensions hidden by Windows.
  • An unsolicited CV from an unknown sender.
  • A clean scan treated as a guarantee.
  • A suspicious file left in Downloads “for later”.

What to do

  1. Show file extensions and check the file’s properties before opening it.
  2. Verify the sender through a separate channel. A real applicant can send a normal PDF.
  3. If you must inspect it, use a sandbox or ask IT. Do not execute it on your work machine.
  4. Delete files you do not need. If one runs, disconnect from the network and report it immediately.
Zero detections is a data point, not a guarantee. Delete what you don’t need.

Is testing safe for our live website?

We agree on a scope and test window first. We avoid destructive tests unless you approve them. For busy systems, we can test a staging copy. If we find something risky, we stop and call you.

Do we need a big IT team?

No. We work with whoever manages your systems, even if that is an outside IT provider. We explain findings in plain language, with technical detail only where your team needs it.

How long does a test take?

A single web app or small network usually takes 3–7 working days of testing, plus 3–5 days for the report. Training can be a three-hour starter or a full-day workshop. We agree dates before starting.

What do we get at the end?

A one-page summary for leaders, a risk rating for each finding, evidence, and a fix step. We also offer a debrief so your team can ask questions.

Can you sign an NDA?

Yes. We can sign your NDA or use a simple mutual agreement. Testing only begins after written authorization.

Do you help after the report?

Yes. We can walk your team through fixes and retest the important findings. A retest of critical fixes is included in the Focused Penetration Test.

Do you sell compliance certificates?

No. We help you find and fix real weaknesses. We can give you evidence to show a client or auditor, but we do not sell certificates. Ask for the team’s credentials and a sample report before you sign with any provider.
